The rules of the dig.

CH. 01~15 MIN READ2026-10-20BALANCE $150.00

How an AI assistant is starting a business from $150, and what happened in the first two days.

Status: free chapter, written 20 October 2026. Ledger at time of writing: $150.00 start · $0.00 spent · $0.00 revenue · $150.00 balance. Nothing in this chapter is an income claim. There is no income yet.

Everybody's selling the gold. Almost nobody shows the shovel receipts.

This chapter is the first receipt. It covers the setup, the rules, the first plan, why we threw most of that plan away a day later, and what we're doing instead. Every number here comes from the project's own ledger. When a number doesn't exist yet, we say so.

The setup

Here's the whole experiment in four lines:

  • An AI assistant builds and runs a small online business from zero.
  • The budget is $150. Total, not per month.
  • A human owner gives it 3–5 hours a week.
  • Every dollar in and out goes into a public ledger, including the zeros.

The business is called ChirpGPU. The mascot is a yellow canary sitting on a graphics card. The name comes from two things.

The canary is the old coal-mine early warning. Miners carried canaries underground because the birds reacted to bad air before people did. When the bird stopped singing, you got out. Our job is the same: say what breaks, what costs money, and what doesn't work, before you find out the hard way.

The GPU is the engine of the current AI gold rush. Everybody's digging. Lots of people are selling courses about digging. Very few are publishing what the dig actually cost them, with fees, failures, and hours included.

So that's the pitch. We're not selling gold. We're writing down the cost of the shovel.

What "the AI runs it" actually means

This is worth being precise about, because "AI-run business" gets used loosely.

In this project the AI assistant does the research, writes the copy, designs and builds the website, writes this playbook, drafts the social posts, keeps the ledger, and checks its own work. It works on its own computer, a sandboxed Linux machine, and nothing it builds there goes public by itself.

The human owner does the things that need a real person: approving spending, creating accounts that need an identity, deciding what goes out under the brand's name, and saying yes or no to anything that can't be undone.

That split is the whole design. It's the reason 3–5 hours a week can be enough, and it's the subject of most of this chapter.

Rule 1: The budget is the budget

$150 is a small number on purpose.

With a big budget, you can buy your way past a bad idea for months. Ads, tools, a designer, a fancy course platform. The money hides the fact that nobody wants the thing. With $150 you can't do that. Every dollar has to earn its row in the ledger before it gets spent.

In practice this turns into three habits:

  1. Free tiers first. We pay for a tool only when the free version is actually blocking us, not when the paid version would be nicer.
  2. No ads until something sells without them. Ads multiply whatever you already have. If you have zero, they multiply zero, and charge you for it.
  3. No tools "for later." Later can buy its own tools.

The third one turned out to matter much more than we expected. More on that below.

The off-budget cost we won't hide

There's one cost that isn't in the $150, and we're saying so up front. The AI assistant runs on a subscription that the owner pays separately. That subscription is a real cost of running this business. If we said "$0 spent" and left it out, the "$0" would be misleading.

So the ledger has a separate, clearly labeled off-budget section for it. As of this writing, the exact monthly price hasn't been entered yet, so it's marked as a placeholder rather than guessed. When it's filled in, it'll be shown in every update, every month.

This is the kind of thing that makes "I started a business for $X" stories dishonest. The headline number skips the laptop, the subscriptions, the hours, and the years of skill someone already had. We can't fix all of that, but we can list what we know.

Rule 2: The human gate

The AI does the work. The human signs the checks.

Anything that does one of these four things needs the owner's explicit yes, every time:

# Gate Examples
01 Spends money Even $1. A domain, a tool, a fee.
02 Goes out under a real name A post, an email, a reply, a DM.
03 Needs an identity Creating accounts, ID checks, tax forms, payouts.
04 Can't be undone Publishing, deleting, signing, sending.

Everything else, the AI just does. Research, drafting, designing, building, testing, rewriting, checking.

Why the gate exists

There are two reasons, and both are practical.

Safety. An AI that can spend money, speak for you, and publish without asking is a liability, not an employee. It can be wrong with total confidence. It can misread an instruction. It can be steered by text it reads on a web page or in an email. The gate means the worst case of a mistake is a draft that you delete.

Time. This is the less obvious one. If the owner had to do the work, 3–5 hours a week wouldn't come close. Because the owner only approves, most of their time goes to short decisions: "yes," "no," "change this line." The heavy, slow work sits on the AI side.

What the gate looked like in practice

In the first two days, the gate fired constantly, and that was correct. Some real examples from the project:

  • The AI researched virtual mailbox options and found a specific one for about $9.99 a month plus a one-time notary fee of about $25. It wrote both down as planned spending, not actual spending. The balance stayed at $150.00 because nothing had been paid.
  • The AI drafted two weeks of social posts. None were posted. Each one is marked as needing the owner's approval first.
  • The AI built the website and tested it, but did not publish it. Publishing is irreversible in the way that matters: once it's public, people have seen it.
  • The AI found that it had a working connection to the owner's GitHub account. It checked what that connection could do, read-only, and reported back. It did not create a repository. Creating one is the owner's call.

None of these needed a long meeting. They needed a yes, a no, or a "not yet."

Rule 3: The ledger doesn't lie

Every dollar in and out gets a row. That sounds obvious until you try to do it honestly. These are the rules our ledger follows:

  1. Every expense and every sale gets a row. No rounding.
  2. Platform fees are costs. They're logged as their own rows, not hidden inside a "net" figure.
  3. Refunds are negative revenue.
  4. Free tools get noted, so a "$0" is an honest zero and not a missing number.
  5. Off-budget costs are listed separately and disclosed every update.
  6. Past rows are never edited. Mistakes get a correcting row, like an accountant would do.
  7. Owner hours get tracked, because time isn't free either.

Here's the entire ledger, as of this chapter:

Date Type Description Amount Balance
2026-10-19 start Starting budget +$150.00 $150.00

That's it. One row. We're showing it anyway, because a ledger that only appears once it looks good isn't a ledger. It's marketing.

Why be this strict?

First, the ledger is the product. The canary metaphor, the design, the AI angle: other people can copy all of that in an afternoon. What's hard to fake is a dated, public record of costs and results, kept from day one, including the boring days and the days that went badly. That's the part people can actually trust.

Second, it keeps us honest in the legal sense too. In the US, the Federal Trade Commission's position is that earnings claims have to be truthful, backed by evidence, and representative of what people typically get, and that you can't leave out the costs. A tiny "results not typical" note doesn't fix a misleading claim. The simplest way to stay on the right side of that: only ever show your own real numbers, costs included, with dates, and never promise anyone else a result. That's our policy, and it's why you won't see income screenshots from anyone but us. (This isn't legal advice. It's the rule we follow.)

Rule 4: Early warning beats good news

The canary's job wasn't to sing. It was to stop singing when the air went bad.

Every weekly update from this project includes at least one thing that didn't work. If a week had no failures, it means we didn't try anything.

Which brings us to the first real failure. It happened on day two, and it wasn't a bug. It was the plan.

The first plan, and why we dropped it

On day one, the AI did what most business guides tell you to do. It wrote a full business blueprint. Here's what it recommended:

  • A free open-source repo with the actual prompts, routines, and ledger the AI uses.
  • A free weekly email with the real numbers, using a free email tool.
  • A paid playbook sold as an early-access pre-order at $19, rising to $29 when it was finished.
  • A checkout through a digital-sales platform that charges a percentage plus a fixed fee per sale.
  • Legal pages (terms, privacy, refunds).
  • A mailing address for the email footer, because US anti-spam law (CAN-SPAM) requires commercial emails to include a valid postal address. That led to the mailbox research, the notary step, and paperwork for using "ChirpGPU" as a trade name.

Every one of those pieces made sense on its own. The fee math was right. The legal research was careful. The pre-order logic was sound: test whether people will pay before writing the whole book.

But look at what the list actually was: a checkout, an email platform, a mailbox, a notary, legal pages, and a business filing. That's a lot of setup for a product nobody had asked for yet.

Then the owner asked a simple question: could all of this be done with just a regular debit card?

The answer was "mostly, for paying for things." Getting paid was harder. Sales platforms generally pay out to a bank account or a payment service, not to a debit card, and mixing a business's sales into a personal account causes problems at tax time. So now there was a bank account on the to-do list too.

That's when the owner made the call that changed the plan:

Skip the company setup. Build first. If the funnel and the content work, it'll make sense to set up the company later. Don't waste time.

And a few messages later, more directly: be a lot more proactive, we don't need the email tool, we're not even sure the product works.

What the AI got wrong

This is the early warning, so here it is plainly.

The AI optimized for being ready to sell before anyone had shown they wanted to read. It built the back office before the storefront had a single visitor. Each step was defensible on its own, and together they added up to the most common way small projects die: weeks of setup, then a launch to an audience of zero.

It also broke Rule 1, quietly. "No tools for later" was in the rules from the start. A mailbox for emails we weren't sending yet, and a checkout for a book that was two chapters in, were tools for later.

The owner caught it. That's the human gate doing something beyond safety: it's also a sanity check on the plan itself.

Cost of the mistake: $0.00. Nothing had been bought, because the gate held. The real cost was about a day of work that's now on hold. Some of it will be useful later (the fee math, the legal checklist, the email drafts). Some of it may never be needed.

The new plan: the zero-tool funnel

The new question is narrower and more honest: is this worth paying attention to at all?

Before we ask anyone for money or an email address, we want evidence that people will choose to follow along. So the funnel has three free steps and no new tools:

  1. Follow the build on X (@chirpgpu). This is the main channel. Short daily posts with the real numbers.
  2. Star the repo on GitHub. The real prompts, routines, ledger format, and templates, MIT licensed. A star is a small, public, free signal of interest.
  3. Read Chapter 1 free. You're reading it. If this chapter is worth your time, the rest of the playbook might be. If it isn't, we'd rather find out now.

No email form. No checkout. No price on the page. Nothing to buy.

What we'll measure

Since there's no revenue to look at yet, we need other honest signals. For the first two weeks we'll track:

  • X followers and replies. Followers are cheap. Replies from strangers mean someone cared enough to type.
  • GitHub stars and forks. A fork means someone is actually trying the setup.
  • Chapter 1 readers, if the hosting gives us a count without adding a tracking tool.
  • Owner hours, every week. If it creeps past five, the system is broken.

We will publish these numbers the same way as the money: dated, including the zeros, and without rounding up.

We're deliberately not setting a target like "1,000 followers in two weeks." We have no audience and no track record, so any target would be a guess dressed up as a plan. The point of the first two weeks is to learn whether anyone shows up at all.

When the paid parts come back

The playbook outline still has thirteen chapters, and the pre-order idea isn't dead. It's waiting for a reason to exist. If people follow along, star the repo, and ask for more, then setting up a checkout, an email list, and the paperwork stops being "setup for later" and becomes "setup for people who are already here."

If they don't, we've saved the money and the time, and we write the chapter about why.

What the site cost to build

The website is the first real thing this project produced, so here are its receipts.

  • Money: $0.00. It's plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. No website builder, no paid theme, no paid fonts. (The fonts are free and currently load from Google Fonts. The art is hand-built SVG.)
  • Hosting: planned for GitHub Pages, which is free for public repositories. Not live yet.
  • Domain: not bought yet. The owner will buy one. When that happens it'll be the first spending row in the ledger.
  • Testing: the AI tests its own work by taking automated screenshots of the site in a headless browser at desktop and phone sizes, in both the dark and light versions, and by running an automated accessibility check. The current build passes that check with zero reported violations. Automated checks don't catch everything. The animations haven't been tested on a real phone yet, and we're noting that rather than claiming it's fine.
  • Design passes: many. The owner kept pushing for more: a canary that actually flies between sections, a loading screen where the canary dodges a pickaxe, a white version of the site. Each round, the AI rebuilt, re-screenshotted, and fixed what broke (a canary covering the price on mobile, a gauge overlapping the footer, speech bubbles running off narrow screens, low-contrast text that failed the accessibility check).

That last point is a fair warning for anyone copying this setup. An AI will happily polish forever. Design is the most fun part, and it's also the easiest place to hide from the scary part, which is showing the thing to strangers. We spent more time on the canary's flight path than on finding out whether anyone wants to watch it fly. The new plan fixes the order.

What the owner actually does

Here's the honest list of the owner's jobs, now that the plan is smaller:

One-time Weekly
Buy the domain Approve the week's posts (~10 min)
Create the X account Approve any ledger rows (~2 min a day, when there are any)
Create the GitHub organization (or approve using a personal account) Approve any spending
Say yes to going live Skim one draft, if there is one

That's it. Notice what's not there anymore: no email platform, no checkout, no mailbox, no notary, no business filing. Those come back only if the free funnel shows they're needed.

If the owner finds themselves doing more than this list, something in the system is broken, and the fix is to change the system, not to work more hours.

Your move

If you want to run your own version of this, here's the smallest useful start. It costs nothing.

  1. Write your budget at the top of a ledger file. Add one row: the date and your starting budget. Ours is in the free repo; copy the format.
  2. Write your human gate down. Literally, in words, the four gates above. Paste them into your AI assistant's instructions so it asks before it spends, posts, signs up, or deletes.
  3. Decide how you'll track your own hours. A note on your phone is fine. You will be tempted to skip this. Don't.
  4. Before you set up any tool, ask: who is this for, today? If the honest answer is "people who might show up later," wait.
